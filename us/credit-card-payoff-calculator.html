How it works — how credit cards compound

Credit cards use daily periodic rate (DPR) compounding: APR ÷ 365 applied to the average daily balance. Unlike a mortgage with fixed amortization, interest is added to the balance when you underpay, and the following month's interest is charged on that larger number — this is the snowball that makes minimum payments so destructive.

This calculator simulates about 30.42 days per month, applying DPR compounding and subtracting your payment until the balance hits zero. If your payment does not exceed the monthly interest charge, the calculator warns you the debt will grow rather than shrink.

Worked example — $5,000 at 24.99% APR

Maya has a $5,000 balance at 24.99% APR. Paying $200/month, daily compounding adds roughly $100/month in interest at the start. It takes 36 months (3 years) to hit zero, costing $2,136 in interest. Her payoff date lands around October 2029.

If Maya adds just $50 extra per month, payoff drops to about 28 months, saving roughly $700 in interest. If she pays the 2% minimum ($100/month initially), payoff stretches beyond 8 years and interest exceeds $4,400 — proof that minimums are designed to keep you paying, not to retire your debt.

Local (US) rules and context

  • The CARD Act 2009 requires minimum payments to cover interest + fees + at least 1% of principal — commonly 2-3% of the total balance.
  • Card issuers must disclose how long the minimum would take to clear your balance — check your statement.
  • Balance-transfer cards with 0% intro APR (typically 12-21 months) can reset the interest clock; a 3-5% transfer fee usually applies.
  • Late-payment penalties: up to roughly $41 late fee, plus possible penalty APR up to 29.99%.
  • Fixed vs variable APR: check whether your rate floats with the prime rate before planning long-term.

Practical tips

  • Avalanche method: pay the highest-APR card first — mathematically saves the most interest.
  • Snowball method: pay the smallest-balance card first (motivation wins), costs more interest.
  • Late payments damage credit for up to 7 years — set autopay at the minimum so you never miss.
  • If you can't afford the minimum, contact your issuer before you miss a payment — hardship programs exist.
  • Cards with active intro-APR offers can charge retroactive back-interest (store cards) once the promo expires — read the fine print.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Paying only the minimum — it builds the card company's revenue schedule, not your freedom date.
  • Opening a new balance-transfer card while spending on the old one.
  • Closing paid-off cards immediately — your credit-utilization ratio jumps and the score can drop.
  • Ignoring annual fees on cards you no longer use — downgrade instead of closing.
  • Assuming a 0% promo lasts forever — circle the expiry date and pay in full before it.

How credit-card interest works

Credit cards compound daily, not monthly. Effective daily rate = APR ÷ 365. The formula this calculator uses: monthly balance × daily-APR compounding, minus your payment, repeated until the balance reaches $0.

Minimum payment trap: if you only pay the minimum (typically 2% of balance), a $5,000 balance at 24.99% APR can take over 20 years and cost thousands in interest. Even +$50/month extra can cut years off your payoff.

Frequently asked questions

What if my payment is less than the monthly interest?

The calculator will warn you — the balance grows rather than shrinks, and you'll never pay it off. Increase your payment above the monthly interest charge.

Does this factor in new purchases on the card?

No. Stop using the card for new purchases if you want this payoff date to hold.

How long does paying off a $5,000 credit card at 24% APR take?

At a 2 percent minimum of about 100 dollars per month, roughly 8 years and over 4,400 dollars in interest. At 200 dollars per month, about 3 years and 2,100 dollars in interest.

Is it better to pay off credit cards or save an emergency fund first?

Keep about 500 to 1,000 dollars as a mini emergency fund, then attack high-APR debt, then rebuild savings. Clearing a 24 percent APR card mathematically beats earning 4-5 percent in savings.

Does making multiple small payments during the month help?

Yes. Interest is calculated on the daily balance, so two 100-dollar payments reduce the average daily balance more than one 200-dollar payment at month-end.

What is a balance transfer and should I use one?

Moving your balance to a card with a 0 percent intro APR, usually 12 to 21 months with a 3-5 percent transfer fee. It saves interest if you clear the balance during the intro window.

Will making extra payments improve my credit score faster?

Lower balances reduce your credit-utilization ratio, a major score factor. The effect usually shows within one to two statement cycles of the lower balance being reported.

Does this calculator work for store cards and gas cards?

Any revolving line with an APR works. Some store cards use deferred-interest promotions that charge retroactive back-interest if the balance is not paid in full by the promo end date.

Are there fees the calculator ignores?

Annual fees, late fees up to roughly 41 dollars, penalty APRs up to 29.99 percent, foreign transaction fees, and cash-advance fees. Any of these change the payoff plan.

What happens if I pay off my card and stop using it?

Keep the card open to preserve credit age and utilization. Most issuers close accounts after 24 or more months of inactivity, so put a small recurring subscription on it and auto-pay in full.
