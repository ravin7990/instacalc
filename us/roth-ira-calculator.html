The Roth IRA Calculator models how after-tax contributions grow 100% tax-free — the only mainstream US retirement account where neither contributions nor qualified withdrawals ever face income tax.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your current age and target retirement age.
  2. Enter your current Roth IRA balance.
  3. Enter your annual contribution (2026 limit: $7,500 under 50 / $8,500 age 50+).
  4. Set an expected annual return (7-8% is a reasonable inflation-adjusted estimate).
  5. Click Calculate — you'll see ending balance, total contributions, and total tax-free growth.

The method behind the math

Same compound-growth math as the 401(k): Balance(t+1) = (Balance(t) + contribution) × (1 + return). The difference is entirely in the tax treatment — contributions come from post-tax dollars today, and qualified withdrawals at 59½+ (account held 5+ years) are completely tax-free, including all growth.

Worked example

At age 30 contributing $7,000/yr for 37 years at 7% annual return, your total contributions are $259,000 — but the ending balance reaches about $965,000. Every dollar of the ~$706,000 in growth is tax-free at withdrawal, saving roughly $210,000 in federal taxes versus a taxable account at a 30% effective rate.

Rules and regulations that affect your result

  • 2026 contribution limit: $7,500 under 50 / $8,500 age 50+ (catch-up).
  • 2026 MAGI phase-out for single filers: starts around $153,000-168,000 (exact figures confirmed annually by IRS); MFJ roughly double.
  • No age limit on contributions since SECURE Act 2019 (you can contribute past 70½, unlike traditional IRAs until recently).
  • No RMDs during the original owner's lifetime — a major estate-planning advantage.
  • Withdrawals of contributions can happen anytime tax- and penalty-free; earnings require age 59½ + 5-year holding.
  • First-time home purchase exception allows up to $10,000 of earnings withdrawal penalty-free.

Practical tips

  • File taxes early and fund the Roth from your refund — "supercharge" the contribution before April 15.
  • Do a "backdoor Roth" if your income exceeds phase-out: contribute to a Traditional IRA non-deductibly, then convert (watch the pro-rata rule if you have pre-tax IRA money).
  • Use Roth for high-growth assets (stocks) — the bigger the growth, the more the tax-free advantage matters.
  • Consider Roth conversions in low-income years (job loss, early retirement gap years).
  • Keep the 5-year clock in mind for each conversion — each has its own holding period.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Exceeding the income limit and triggering a 6% annual excess-contribution penalty.
  • Withdrawing earnings before 59½ + 5 years — taxes plus the 10% early-withdrawal penalty.
  • Confusing Roth IRA with Roth 401(k) — different contribution limits and RMD rules.
  • Forgetting the 5-year rule applies separately to each conversion.
  • Funding a Roth before getting the full employer 401(k) match — match beats Roth growth.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 2026 Roth IRA contribution limit?

7,500 dollars under age 50, 8,500 dollars at age 50 and older with the catch-up. The limit applies to combined Traditional plus Roth IRA contributions.

What is the income limit for contributing?

For 2026, the single-filer MAGI phase-out begins around 153,000-168,000 dollars; for MFJ it is roughly double. Above the upper limit you cannot contribute directly — use a backdoor Roth.

What is a backdoor Roth?

Contribute non-deductibly to a Traditional IRA (no income limit), then convert to Roth. Watch the pro-rata rule — if you hold pre-tax IRA money anywhere, a portion of the conversion is taxable.

When can I withdraw without penalty?

Contributions can be withdrawn anytime, tax- and penalty-free. Earnings require age 59½ and an account held at least 5 years. Exceptions include first home (up to 10,000 dollars of earnings), disability, and qualified education expenses.

Are Roth IRAs subject to RMDs?

No — no Required Minimum Distributions during the original owner's lifetime. This is a major estate-planning advantage over traditional IRAs and 401(k)s.

Can I convert a traditional IRA to Roth?

Yes — a Roth conversion is taxable on the pre-tax portion converted. Many do "ladder" conversions in low-income years (retirement gap years before RMD age) to permanently minimize lifetime taxes.

Tax-free growth: does that mean no tax on dividends?

Correct — dividends, interest, and capital gains inside a Roth IRA are never taxed, even at withdrawal, as long as it is a qualified distribution.

What is the 5-year rule?

Earnings must have been in a Roth account at least 5 tax years before a qualified (tax- and penalty-free) withdrawal of earnings at 59½. Each conversion has its own separate 5-year clock.

Roth IRA or traditional IRA?

Roth when you expect to be in a higher tax bracket at retirement, traditional when lower. Most young workers are better served by Roth — locking today's low rates before tax brackets rise on large balances.

Can my spouse contribute without earned income?

Yes — a "spousal IRA" contribution up to the annual limit based on the working spouse's earned income, filed jointly, as both partners' combined IRA contributions stay under the limit.
