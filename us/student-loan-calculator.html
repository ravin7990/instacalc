The method — amortization plus daily accrual

Federal and private student loans use a standard amortization variation: monthly rate = APR/12, fixed payment by term, interest paid first and principal second. Most student loans also accrue interest daily (APR ÷ 365.25) on the outstanding balance — meaning interest starts the day funds are disbursed, unless the loan is subsidized.

The amortized monthly payment follows the classic formula: M = P × r / (1 − (1+r)^−n), where P is principal, r the monthly rate, and n the number of payments. This calculator also models an early-payoff scenario with your extra payment, reporting months saved and total interest reduction.

Worked example — $35,000 at 5.5% / 10 years

Aiden graduated with $35,000 in Direct Unsubsidized loans at 5.5%. Standard 10-year repayment: $380/month, $10,581 interest, total repaid $45,581.

If Aiden adds $100 extra per month, payoff drops to about 89 months (7.4 years), saving roughly $2,880 in interest. Stretch to a 25-year extended plan and the payment drops to about $213, but total interest balloons to around $28,900 — a 173% increase for the relief of $167/month.

Local (US) rules and context

  • Federal rates reset every July 1 based on the 10-year Treasury auction — check studentaid.gov for current-year rates.
  • Subsidized vs unsubsidized: subsidized loans don't accrue interest during school; unsubsidized accrue from disbursement.
  • Income-Driven Repayment (SAVE, PAYE, IBR, ICR) recomputes payments based on income and family size, and can extend to 20-25 years with eventual forgiveness — this calculator doesn't model IDR.
  • Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) requires 120 qualifying payments at a qualifying employer.
  • Consolidation can lower payments by extending term but increases total interest.
  • Deferment/forbearance pauses payments, but unsubsidized loans keep accruing interest.
  • Private loans have lender-set rates, often tied to credit; no federal forgiveness applies.

Practical tips

  • Pay interest during school on unsubsidized loans, even small amounts — it prevents capitalization at graduation.
  • Auto-pay typically saves 0.25% on the interest rate with most servicers.
  • Target the highest-rate loan first if you have multiple — the avalanche approach beats the snowball mathematically.
  • Recertify income annually if on IDR — a missed recertification causes a payment shock.
  • Refinancing loses federal protections (IDR, PSLF, deferment) — only refinance if you're certain you won't need them.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming the grace period is interest-free — unsubsidized loan interest accrues during the 6-month grace after graduation.
  • Forgetting annual IDR income recertification, causing a payment shock.
  • Refinancing federal loans for a lower rate and unknowingly losing PSLF eligibility.
  • Confusing capitalized vs non-capitalized interest on subsidized loans.
  • Ignoring the SAVE-plan litigation status — verify current IDR rules on studentaid.gov before enrolling.

How student-loan interest works

Federal student loans (Direct subsidized and unsubsidized) use a daily interest accrual of rate ÷ 365.25 added to your balance. Standard repayment is 10 years, extended plans can stretch to 25 years for balances above $30,000, and income-driven repayment (IDR) plans set monthly payments based on discretionary income, not amortization.

Verify current rates on the Federal Student Aid (studentaid.gov) site — rates reset every July.

Frequently asked questions

Does this include income-driven repayment (IDR)?

No. This calculator amortizes like a standard or extended repayment plan. IDR plans recompute payments each year based on income and family size, so amortization math doesn't apply.

Are loan forgiveness programs included?

No. Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF), Teacher Loan Forgiveness, and income-based forgiveness are separate programs; this calculator assumes the full balance is repaid.

How accurate is this calculator for private student loans?

For fixed-rate private loans the math is the same. Variable-rate private loans change the payment every rate adjustment, so treat the result as a snapshot at today's rate.

What is the current federal student loan interest rate?

Undergraduate Direct loan rates reset every July 1 based on the 10-year Treasury auction — verify the current-year figure at studentaid.gov rather than assuming a fixed number.

Do I save money by paying extra each month?

Yes. Extra payments go straight to principal, reducing the interest that accrues afterwards. On a $35,000 loan at 5.5 percent, an extra $100 per month saves roughly $2,880 in interest and shortens payoff by about 2.5 years.

Should I refinance my student loans?

Only if you have stable income and don't need federal protections (IDR, PSLF, deferment). Refinancing federal loans into a private loan permanently gives up those protections.

What happens during a deferment or forbearance?

Payments pause, but unsubsidized and private loans keep accruing interest. Subsidized federal loans may not accrue interest during deferment depending on the loan type and economic hardship status.

Can I deduct student-loan interest on my taxes?

Yes — the Student Loan Interest Deduction allows up to $2,500 per taxpayer per year, with income limits (MAGI phase-out; check current IRS rules). You don't need to itemize to claim it.

What is loan capitalization?

Unpaid accrued interest gets added to the principal balance (capitalized) at certain events — end of grace period, end of deferment, IDR recertification events. It increases the balance you pay interest on going forward.

Does the standard repayment term have to be 10 years?

Federal Direct loans default to 10-year standard repayment. Extended plans of 15, 20, or 25 years are available for balances above roughly $30,000, but longer terms pay more total interest.
